## C03 evidence dossier (primary language)

### 1. Petition language (July 1593) — the organising self-account

The petition (TNA **SP 63/170 f. 204** and related calendar tradition) is the
clearest recoverable primary phrasing behind C03. Published paraphrases and
transcriptions converge on the following sense and often preserve early-modern
spelling:

> … the continual discord stirs and dissention which heretofore long time
> remained among the Irish especially in West Connaught by the sea-side, every
> chieftain for his safeguard and maintainance and for the defence of his people,
> followers and country took arms by strong hand to make head against his
> neighbours, who in like manner constrained your highness fond subject to take
> arms and by force to maintain herself and her people by sea and land the space
> of forty years past.

*(Composite of calendar/secondary transcriptions widely reproduced from the 1593
petition tradition; treat as close paraphrase pending a diplomatic check against
the folio / calendar entry used for release.)*

Murray (*History Ireland*) quotes a compressed version of the same justification:

> ‘discord . . . and dissention . . . [where] every chieftain . . . took arms by
> strong hand to make head against his neighbours which in like manner
> constrained your highness fond subject to take arms and by force to maintain
> herself and her people’.

**What this actually says (high confidence):**

- She frames forty years of armed action as **forced by neighbour-chieftain
  discord**, not as a romantic vocation.
- The key couplet is **maintain herself and her people by sea and land**.
- “By force” / “took arms” sits inside the same sentence as maintenance: livelihood
  and coercion are not separated in her self-presentation.

**What this does *not* say:**

- It does not enumerate “trade,” “tolls,” and “seizure” as three named occupations.
- It does not confess “piracy” as an English legal category she accepts.
- It does not describe a day-on-the-water scene.

### 2. Interrogatory answers — “trade of maintenance by sea and land”

Burghley’s eighteen articles ask after family, lands, and **maintenance**. A
widely reprinted answer to the question of how she lived since her last husband’s
death (Chambers transcription tradition; scoilnet educational extract) includes:

> After the death of her last husband she gathered together all her own followers
> and with 1,000 head of cows and mares departed and became a dweller in
> Carrigahowley … Ever since she dwelleth in Connaught a farmer’s life very poor
> … utterly did she give over her former trade of maintenance by sea and land.

*(Wording as printed in secondary educational materials quoting Chambers /
State Papers; verify against SP 63/170 answer pages before release quotation.)*

**What this adds (high confidence for sense; medium for exact orthography):**

- She herself calls the earlier livelihood a **“trade of maintenance by sea and
  land.”** That is period self-language available to the app.
- In 1593 she claims to have **given it over** and to be living poorly as a
  farmer — a petitioning posture under Bingham pressure (links C03 → C08).
- Land wealth (cattle/mares) and Rockfleet residence appear in the same answer
  sequence: maintenance is not sea-only.

### 3. Petition asks — maintenance restored as royal favour

Wikipedia and calendar-based secondary literature quote petition requests that
match the source brief’s L1 paraphrase:

- “some reasonable maintenance for the little tyme she hath to live”
- “free libertye during her lyve to envade with sword and fire all your highness
  enemyes …”

**Learner-safe reading (high):** she asks the Queen both for **support to live on**
and for **licence to take armed action against the Queen’s enemies** — i.e. she
tries to re-legitimise force as service. Do not collapse this into “permission to
be a pirate.”

### 4. How far “tolling” and “seizure” go inside C03

| Element | Status in primary self-account | Status in wider English record / scholarly synthesis | App posture |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Maintenance by land and sea | **Her words** | Confirmed across petition + answers | L1 core |
| Force / taking arms | **Her words** | Hostile officials intensify into spoil/theft/rebellion | L1 core; keep purpose visible |
| “Trade” of maintenance | **Her words** (“former trade of…”) | Chambers/Murray synthesise trade + mercenary carriage + plunder | Prefer her phrase over modern “commerce” |
| Tolls / levies on shipping | **Not named in the famous petition sentence** | Strong in secondary synthesis (Murray: O’Malleys levied toll on shipping in their waters; Chambers/RMG: toll for safe passage; History Guild/TNA tradition: levies or plunder) | **Story-safe with hedge**: “control of routes could support trade and the taking of tolls” (current draft is good); do not put “she said she took tolls” on L1 |
| Opportunistic seizure / spoil | Implied by “by force”; not itemised | Hostile: spoiler, thieves and murderers at sea, spoil of cattle/goods; her later answers narrate being bound/stripped | Keep seizure visible **without romance**; attribute hostile labels as hostile |
| Pure merchant trade | Not claimed as exclusive | Chambers: part trade (fish, hides, etc. ↔ iron, wine), part mercenary, part piracy | Never sanitise the chapter into “trade only” |

**Confidence summary for C03:** the chapter’s moral centre — *do not sanitise into
pure trade* — is historically correct. The petition’s own language already joins
**force** to **maintenance**. Tolling is historically plausible and widely
attested in careful secondary work on Uí Mháille practice; it should be taught as
**coastal practice under local authority**, not as a recovered 1593 confession.

## Hostile-source framing guidance

English officials are not neutral reporters of a maritime CV. Keep bias visible
whenever their words carry a beat.

### Recoverable hostile / official phrases (bias kept on)

| Speaker / context | Recoverable language (secondary transcription tradition) | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sir Henry Sidney (recognition + stigma) | “most famous feminine sea-captain”; “three galleys and 200 fighting men”; “notorious woman in all the coasts of Ireland”; “more than Mrs Mate” with her husband | Admits real force while marking her as anomalous and notorious |
| Lord Justice Drury (1570s captivity trail) | “impudently past the part of womanhood”; “great spoiler and chief commander and director of thieves and murderers at sea” | Gender + criminalisation frame |
| Sir Richard Bingham (1593 pressure) | “nurse to all rebellions in the province for [this] forty years”; “notable traitress”; woman who “overstepped the part of womanhood”; asks ships to “beate her … boates and Galleys” | Makes her the standing cause of provincial disorder; justifies hard presidency |
| Later / related state-paper voice | “hath at times lived out of order” (Murray summary of how some charges were softened at court) | Even royal process can reframe rather than fully endorse |

### Framing rules for Chapter 4 (and Ch5 handoff)

1. **Her petitioning voice first** when teaching *how she said the system worked*
   (maintenance / force / trade of maintenance).
2. **Hostile voice second**, labelled as purpose-driven: officials needed her to
   look like disorder so that suppression looked like government.
3. **Do not average the two into “the truth.”** They disagree in purpose, not only
   in tone (current `mayo.power-at-sea.seizure` detail is correct).
4. **Never promote “pirate queen”** as what they called her in period title form
   (C18). Period words are sea captain, notorious woman, spoiler, nurse of
   rebellions, traitress — and her own *maintenance*.
5. Sidney’s “famous” and “notorious” can sit in one sentence: admiration and stigma
   are not opposites in Tudor official prose.

## C04 dependency for this chapter

### What is secure without a pinned cite (**high**)

- An English Lord Deputy, Sir Henry Sidney, recorded that she offered service with
  **galleys and fighting men**.
- He marks her as a **feminine / female sea captain** and as **notorious** on the
  coasts.
- That recognition is widely printed from State Paper tradition and is strong
  enough for a soft L1/L2 beat of *official notice of material sea power*.

### Representative wording variants (do not freeze one until L3)

Secondary and educational publications print close variants, e.g.:

> There came to me also a most famous feminine sea-captain called Grany Imallye
> [Granny Imallye / Grace Imallye], and offered her services unto me,
> wheresoever I would command her, with three galleys and 200 fighting men,
> either in Scotland or Ireland; she brought with her her husband for she was as
> well by sea as by land well more than Mrs. Mate with him … This was a
> notorious woman in all the coasts of Ireland.

### Citation pin still open (why D-F / source brief still flag this)

| Open question | Why it matters |

## Vocabulary / framing: trade, toll, seizure, maintenance, galley

| Term | Learner-safe meaning | Prefer | Avoid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Maintenance** | Livelihood and the means to keep people, followers, and authority going | Her phrase: maintenance by land and sea; trade of maintenance | Modern “salary,” “career,” “business model” |
| **Trade** | Exchange of goods *and*, in her papers, the broader “trade of” making a living by force at sea | “Trade” only with adjacent force/toll language; or quote “trade of maintenance” | Sanitised “peaceful commerce” as the whole story |
| **Toll** | Payment / levy taken under claim of local control of waters or passage | “Tolls from vessels that passed” as practice under local authority | “Pirate tax” as joke; presenting toll as her 1593 self-word |
| **Seizure** | Taking by force — spoils, goods, cattle, sometimes ships | “Opportunistic seizure”; “taking” | Adventure-raid UI; “pirate queen”; learner agency as raider |
| **Galley** | Period English name for the oared/sail coastal war–cargo vessels officials keep mentioning | Soft: “galleys” as the ships officials named; Irish headword stays *long* | Over-specific Viking-longship lecture; claiming one exact build as proven |
| **Long** | Ship (chapter headword) | Use *long* for production sentences | Forcing *bád* / technical Irish ship taxonomy into the cleared 20 |
| **Pirate / piracy** | Modern and hostile English frame | Hostile-voice pages only, labelled | Period title; chapter title; L1 identity |
| **Sea captain / leader** | Official recognition category | Preferred L1 identity language (C18) | “Queen of the seas” as documentary fact |

### Galley technology / Connacht practice (learner-safe ceiling)

Supportable for closer look or restrained story colour (**medium** confidence on
technical detail; **high** that “galley” is the period English vessel word):

- Oared and sail-driven; one English report of a Connacht galley “rowed with
  thirty oars” and carrying substantial armed men (1601 tradition, also used for
  C15 — keep soft).
- Shallow enough for coastal work; useful for movement of men, goods, and force
  among islands and shore castles.
- Size reports vary: Sidney-offer tradition (~200 fighting men with three
  galleys); later papers claim very large manning figures for O’Malley-linked
  galleys — **do not treat any single number as settled L1 fact** until C04/C15
  pins.
- Chambers synthesis: mixed maritime “empire” — trade, carriage/mercenary
  movement, toll, plunder. Murray: O’Malleys earned from land and sea — trade,
  ferrying, fishing, plunder, toll.

**Chapter 4 production rule:** teach the *system* (routes + authority + force +
household), not a shipwright’s reconstruction.
