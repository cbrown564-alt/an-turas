## 1. Executive finding

**What can be asserted with high confidence**

1. The famous description of Gráinne as a “most famous feminine sea captain” who offered **three galleys and 200 fighting men** is **primary text**, not a modern paraphrase. It appears in Sir Henry Sidney’s retrospective memoir of his Irish service, addressed to Sir Francis Walsingham and dated from Ludlow Castle **1 March 1582** (Old Style) / **1 March 1583** (New Style calendar year).
2. In that passage Sidney also calls her a “notorious woman in all the coast of Ireland,” names her **Grany Imallye**, identifies her husband as Richard-in-Iron of the “nether Burkes” (later Mac William Iochtar), and says **Sir Philip Sidney saw and spoke with her**.
3. The memoir places the meeting in the narrative of Sidney’s **third deputyship** (he landed mid-September 1575), during a second appearance at **Galway** after the Clanricarde sons’ revolt, and immediately before news of the Earl of Essex’s fatal illness — chronologically **1576**, not a separate 1577 despatch of this wording.
4. Recoverable manuscript / calendar anchors:
   - Lambeth Palace Library, Carew MSS **MS 601, p. 89** (calendar abstract: Brewer & Bullen, *Calendar of the Carew Manuscripts*, vol. II, doc. **501**).
   - Parallel State Papers Domestic copy: TNA **SP 12/159**; public TNA education use cites the Gráinne passage at **SP 12/159 f. 27d** (name underlined in the manuscript image described there).

**What must not be overstated**

- This is **Sidney’s later recollection** (winter 1582–83), written to justify his career and debts to Walsingham — not a day-book entry from the Galway camp. Recognition is contemporary in *persona* (the Lord Deputy who met her), but the surviving *text* is a recapitulation ~six–seven years later.
- Wikipedia and some popular pages mislabel the passage as a **1577 letter from Henry to Philip Sidney**. The recoverable primary text is **Henry → Walsingham**, mentioning that Philip can tell Walsingham more. Treat the “letter to Philip” framing as a secondary error.
- Secondary literature often dates the *meeting* as 1576 **or** 1577. Internal memoir chronology plus Essex’s death (September 1576) strongly favours **1576**; Chambers’s 1981 article says 1577 while her book extracts often say 1576. For L1, prefer “years before 1593” / “in the mid-1570s” unless L3 carries the pin.
- Theresa Murray’s *History Ireland* addition — that Sidney sailed with her to inspect Galway’s seaward defences and that she billed him — is **not** in the recoverable Sidney memoir sentence-block. Do not promote it to L1/L2 until a separate primary is pinned.
- “~200” in the claim ledger is slightly softer than the primary: Sidney writes an exact **200**, not an approximation. The tilde is only useful if the app wants to signal that the figure is *her offer as reported by Sidney*, not an independently counted muster.

**Verdict for the pack:** C04 is **story-safe** once L1 stays within “sea captain / galleys / fighting men / English official recognition.” Exact “three galleys and 200 fighting men,” name form **Grany Imallye**, and the Carew / SP 12/159 trail belong in **L2–L3**. Draft Chapter 1 and Chapter 4 copy is already cautious and largely sound; the open “citation under review” hedge can now be replaced with a pinned citation.

## 2. Primary source trail

### 2.1 The document (what it is)

Sidney’s text is a long **apologia / memoir** of his Irish (and Welsh) service, framed as a letter to **Sir Francis Walsingham**. Catalogue titles typically run: *“SIR HENRY SYDNEY to SIR FRANCIS WALSINGHAM”* with the gloss *“(A summary relation of all his services in Ireland.)”*

Modern scholarship treats it as a **political memoir** composed in winter **1582–83**. Authoritative modern edition:

- Ciarán Brady (ed.), *A Viceroy’s Vindication? Sir Henry Sidney’s Memoir of Service in Ireland, 1556–78* (Cork University Press, 2000/2002).

Brady and the Irish Manuscripts Commission note that Sidney’s autograph draft is **not known to survive**, but manuscript copies do:

| Copy | Location | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Carew MSS | Lambeth Palace Library, **MS 601**, from f./p. **89**; also cited MS **628** | Calendar abstract is the most widely quoted public transcription |
| State Papers Domestic | TNA **SP 12/159** | Basis of mid-19th-c. printed editions; TNA education cites **f. 27d** for the Gráinne passage |

**Dating the letter:** Carew calendar abstract ends “Ludlow Castle, **1 March 1582**. Signed.” Discovery catalogue dates the same item **1 March 1583**. Both are consistent with English Old Style (year change at 25 March): 1 March 1582 OS = 1 March 1583 NS. Prefer citing **1 March 1583** with a note on OS, or “1 March 1582/3.”

**Not State Papers Ireland (SP 63):** the famous recognition text lives in the **Domestic** series (SP 12) / Carew copies, because it is a retrospective written in England/Wales to the Principal Secretary — not an Irish provincial despatch filed in SP 63.

### 2.2 Printed / calendar access (recoverable wording)

1. **Calendar of the Carew Manuscripts**, ed. J. S. Brewer & William Bullen, vol. II (1575–1588), document **501** — fullish abstract including the Gráinne paragraph (publicly mirrored via TNA Discovery description for the Lambeth item).
2. Mid-19th-century print from SP 12/159: H. F. Hore (ed.), “Sir Henry Sidney’s Memoir of His Government of Ireland,” *Ulster Journal of Archaeology*, 1st ser., III (1855) and continuations — based on TNA SP 12/159 (per IMC calendar introduction).
3. Arthur Collins (ed.), *Letters and Memorials of State* (London, 1746) — early printed Sidney-family corpus often cited in older secondary work; treat as a further print tradition of the same memoir, not an independent witness.
4. Brady’s Cork UP edition — preferred modern scholarly text for board reading (paywalled / purchase; not re-collated line-by-line in this research pass).

### 2.3 Meeting context inside the memoir (why 1576)

In the Carew abstract, Sidney narrates:

- Third landing as Deputy: **14 September 1575**.
- Progress through Munster into Thomond, then **Galway**; Clanricarde’s sons submit, are rebuked in church, then revolt.
- Sidney returns toward the west; summons Clanricarde’s followers to **Galway**.
- **Then** the Gráinne paragraph.
- Immediately after: first news of the Earl of Essex’s “extreme and hopeless sickness”; Philip Sidney hurries and finds him dead in Dublin Castle.

Walter Devereux, 1st Earl of Essex, died **22 September 1576**. That anchors the Galway episode (and thus the meeting) in **1576**.

Secondary sources that say “1577” usually either (a) round the mid-1570s vaguely, (b) slide toward Gráinne’s subsequent Desmond capture / Drury imprisonment sequence (1577–78), or (c) repeat Chambers’s 1981 article dating without re-checking the memoir sequence.

### 2.4 What this pass did *not* recover

- A separate **1576 contemporary despatch** containing the same “three galleys and 200 men” sentence (it may exist among SP 63 progress letters; not located here).
- Diplomatic facsimile of SP 12/159 f. 27d or MS 601 (only calendar/education descriptions).
- Line-perfect collation of Brady vs Carew calendar vs Collins spelling variants.
- Mary O’Dowd ODNB full text (subscription); Wikipedia attributes a misframed “letter to Philip” while citing O’Dowd — **do not treat Wikipedia’s framing as O’Dowd’s wording** without checking the ODNB entry itself.

## 3. Exact recoverable wording

### 3.1 Carew calendar transcription (nearest public full paragraph)

From the Carew Manuscripts calendar abstract of Sidney → Walsingham (Lambeth MS 601; calendar vol. II, doc. 501), as transcribed on TNA Discovery:

> There came to me also a most famous feminine sea captain called **Grany Imallye**, and offered her service unto me wheresoever I would command her, with **three galleys and 200 fighting men** either in Ireland or Scotland; she brought with her her husband, for she was as well by sea as by land more than **Mrs. Mate** with him; he was of the nether Burkes, and now as I hear Mack William Euter, and called by nickname **Richard in Iron**. This was a **notorious woman in all the coast of Ireland**. This woman did **Sir Philip Sydney** see and speak withal; he can more at large inform you of her.

**Caveats on this quote**

- It is a **19th-century calendar modernisation / expansion**, not a diplomatic transcript of every letter-form. Spelling of *Imallye / O’Mallye / I-Mallye*, *galleys / gallyes*, *coast / coasts*, and “Mrs. Mate” vs “master’s mate” varies across prints.
- “Mrs. Mate” is Sidney’s joke on marital hierarchy (she is more than mate to her husband by sea and land) — not a title.
- “Mack William Euter” = Mac William Iochtar; the “now as I hear” clause is written from **1583** knowledge (Richard-an-Iarainn’s later status), looking back on a 1576 meeting.

### 3.2 Common secondary orthography (Chambers et al.)

Widely reprinted (Chambers, *Études irlandaises* 1981; graceomalley.com extract; book jacket tradition), with small variants:

> There came to me also a most famous feminine sea-captain called Grany Imallye, and offered her services unto me, wheresoever I would command her, with three galleys and 200 fighting men, either in Scotland or Ireland; she brought with her her husband for she was as well by sea as by land well more than Mrs. Mate with him… This was a notorious woman in all the coasts of Ireland.

Differences from Carew calendar abstract are orthographic / word-order (“services” plural; Scotland/Ireland swapped; “coasts” plural). **Do not treat any one modernised spelling as the diplomatic form** until a board historian compares Brady / manuscript image.

### 3.3 Numerical claims in the primary block

| Claim | In Sidney memoir text? | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “Sea captain” / “feminine sea captain” | **Yes** | “most famous feminine sea captain” |
| Galleys | **Yes** | “three galleys” |
| Fighting men | **Yes** | “200 fighting men” (exact) |
| Offer of service in Ireland or Scotland | **Yes** | |
| Husband present / she dominates him | **Yes** | “Mrs. Mate” joke |
| Philip Sidney met her | **Yes** | |
| ~200 as soft modern estimate | **No** | Secondary softens; primary is exact 200 |
| Fleet larger than three / standing army of 200 always | **Not in this text** | This is what she *offered* on that occasion as Sidney reports it |
| Galleys that “carry 300 men apiece” | **No — later papers** | Chambers cites separate late-1590s / 1599 notices about Tibbott’s / O’Malley galleys |

**Therefore:** “~200 men often cited in secondary literature” is **understating** the primary: secondary literature cites ~200 **because Sidney wrote 200**. Later “300 men apiece” figures are a **different** documentary strand and must not be collapsed into C04.

## 4. Secondary literature treatment

### 4.1 Anne Chambers

- 1981 article (*Études irlandaises*): leads with the epithet; dates Sidney’s description to **1577**; quotes the three galleys / 200 men passage; treats State Papers as proof she was indeed that sea captain.
- Book tradition (*Granuaile* / *Grace O’Malley*): jacket and extracts often date the same quote **1576**; popularise “Granny Imallye” spelling; correctly keep three + 200 as Sidney’s words.
- **Use:** best narrative synthesis; date 1576 vs 1577 needs the memoir pin above; not a substitute for Carew / SP 12/159 citation in L3.

### 4.2 Theresa D. Murray, *History Ireland* (2005)

- Dates the meeting to Sidney’s **1576** Galway summons (aligns with memoir chronology).
- Quotes / paraphrases the feminine sea captain, three galleys, two hundred men, and “notorious woman” lines.
- **Adds** that Sidney did not take up the military offer but **sailed with her to inspect Galway’s seaward defences**, for which she **successfully billed him**.
- **Status of the addition:** repeated widely online by copying Murray; **not found** in the Carew calendar memoir paragraph recovered here. Flag as **secondary claim awaiting its own primary** (possible lost progress letter or Galway corporation / payment record). Exclude from L1; keep out of L2 until pinned.

### 4.3 Mary O’Dowd, ODNB “O’Malley, Gráinne (fl. 1577–1597)”

- Listed in the source brief as a board cross-check; full text not retrieved this pass (subscription).
- Wikipedia cites O’Dowd while wrongly calling the quote a **letter to Philip in 1577**. Until ODNB is read, treat that Wikipedia sentence as **contaminated** and do not import its framing into L3.

### 4.4 Eleanor Hull / Library Ireland tradition

- Prints a close version of the quote; associates it with Galway submissions; includes the Philip Sidney coda. Useful popular corroboration of the Carew text, not an independent MS.

### 4.5 Encyclopedia / popular synthesis

- Encyclopedia.com and similar correctly link Sidney → Walsingham and quote three galleys / 200 / Mrs Mate / notorious woman, but often date the meeting **1577**.
- Royal Museums Greenwich / History Extra pieces sometimes **paraphrase** Sidney (“famous for her stoutness…”) — do not use those paraphrases as Sidney’s words (Drury’s later description of her “stoutness of courage” is a different document).

## 5. Assessment of “~200 men” and “galleys”

### 5.1 Galleys

- **Primary for C04:** Sidney reports she offered **three galleys**. Period English “galley” on the western Irish coast means oared/hybrid coastal war–transport craft (not necessarily Mediterranean galleys). Chambers discusses typology from other State Paper notices (thirty oars, shot aboard, etc.) — useful L2 colour if clearly marked as **later / other papers**, not Sidney’s three.
- Draft L1 “a woman with galleys” is accurate and appropriately non-numeric.

### 5.2 Two hundred fighting men

- **Primary:** exact figure in Sidney’s memoir as **the force she offered**.
- It is **not** independently audited; it is what Sidney says she claimed she could bring.
- Secondary literature’s “~200” usually means this Sidney figure (sometimes blurred with later 300-per-galley notices). For An Turas:
  - Prefer **“200 fighting men”** when quoting or closely paraphrasing Sidney.
  - Prefer **“fighting men”** without a number on bare L1 if the product wants to avoid implying a standing census.
  - Avoid collapsing Sidney’s 200 with 1599 “300 men apiece.”

### 5.3 “Recognised as sea captain”

Safe: an English Lord Deputy **described** her as a famous feminine sea captain and treated her as someone who could offer ships and men.

Unsafe: that “sea captain” was a formal Tudor office, or that Sidney permanently employed her fleet.

## 9. One-line board answer

**C04 is pin-able:** Sidney’s **1583** memoir to Walsingham (Carew MS 601 / SP 12/159) recalls meeting **Grany Imallye** in **1576** and calls her a **most famous feminine sea captain** who offered **three galleys and 200 fighting men**; “~200” is not a vague modern guess — it is Sidney’s number for her offer, best kept exact when quoted and softened only when the surface copy refuses a census-like figure.
