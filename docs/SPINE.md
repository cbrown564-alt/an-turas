# The Historical Spine

*The behind-the-scenes language and time sequence for the county journey. The learner
travels through 32 counties; this spine makes sure each county story introduces Irish
in an intentional order. The legacy 13-chapter outline remains a useful production and
grammar rail, not the app's primary map. See `COUNTY-ATLAS.md` and D12. Drafted
2026-07-04; reframed 2026-07-10 — needs historian and Irish-language-pedagogue review
before production.*

## How this relates to the county journey

A **county story** is the learner-facing unit: one county, one named real anchor, one
significant reading/encounter, and 20 useful words. A **spine chapter** is a
behind-the-scenes sequencing group: it says which era, grammar, artifact pattern, and
review needs a county story can draw on. It is valid for a county to receive a second
story in a later era; completion means all shipped stories in that county are complete.

The first four existing production chapters map cleanly to Mayo, Offaly, Dublin, and
Meath. Their JSON and progress must be preserved through the story-first migration.
Where an outline below still uses a generic learner-facing character, treat it as an
obsolete drafting device: production copy must use the real anchor in `COUNTY-ATLAS.md`
and `COUNTY-STORY-SLATE.md`.

## Design rules

1. **The county story needs the words.** Every grammar concept enters because a real
   place and its anchor demand it, never as an abstract unit. The era is chosen to
   *fit the grammar*, not decorated onto it; a generic historical stand-in cannot be
   the headline hook.
2. **Explain, then drill.** Each chapter has 2–3 "An Nóta Gramadaí" moments that say
   plainly what school never explained (copula vs *bí*, why mutations exist, what the
   fada does) — written for re-learners, safe for true beginners.
3. **Artifacts, not points.** Each county story ends with a collectible artifact in the
   learner's *músaem* — personalised where possible (your name, your county, your
   surname). The final artifact of the course is the Fáinne.
4. **Past meets present.** Every county story's closing beat connects its era to something
   the learner can see or do today (a real stone in a Mayo field, a placename on a
   road sign, a Belfast mural).
5. **Dialect-variable items are tagged** in content from day one (D2): Connacht
   forms ship first; Ulster/Munster variants slot in later without rewriting.
6. **Review wears the county's clothes.** SRS runs underneath everything, but
   returning material is framed as revisiting places and people, never as card debt.

## The chapters

### 1. Clocha na nAinmneacha — The Stones of Names (Mayo; c. 400–550)

- **Anchor and reading:** the north Mayo ogham stones, especially Breastagh. Read the
  marks, transliteration, and the names they carry; learn what can and cannot be
  inferred from an inscription.
- **Hook:** A stone on the Mayo coast preserves a person's name across fifteen
  centuries. To understand what it says, you first learn how to say who you are.
- **Language:** *Dia dhuit / Dia is Muire dhuit* (call-and-response), *Is mise…*,
  *Cé thusa?*, *Cé as thú?* [dialect-tagged], counties, *mac/iníon* and the shape of
  Irish surnames (Ó/Mac/Ní/Nic), broad/slender vowels and the fada.
- **Grammar notes:** the two "to be"s (identity *is* vs state *tá*), preview only;
  *caol le caol, leathan le leathan*; why MAQI on stones became Mac in your surname.
- **Artifact:** your name carved in ogham. **Seanfhocal:** *Is buaine focal ná toice
  an tsaoil* — a word outlasts the world's riches.
- **Present-day beat:** the Breastagh ogham stone still stands in a field near
  Killala. The words outlasted everyone who carved them.

### 2. Oileán na Naomh — Island of Saints and Scholars (Offaly; 550–800)

- **Anchor and reading:** St Ciarán and Clonmacnoise. Enter a real learning-place on
  the Shannon through an early Life of Ciarán and the Cross of the Scriptures.
- **Hook:** At dawn in Ciarán's monastery, the daily work of a great learning-place
  begins: pigments, vellum, meals, prayer, and a page that still speaks across time.
- **Language:** *tá* + VSO present tense, daily routine verbs, telling time by the
  monastic hours, numbers 1–10, colours (the inks: corcra from lichen, gorm from
  woad), food and *is maith liom / ní maith liom*.
- **Grammar notes:** VSO — why the verb comes first and why that's freeing, not
  weird; the difference between *tá mé* and *is mise* now made explicit.
- **Artifact:** your initial as an illuminated letter. **Seanfhocal:** *Ní neart go
  cur le chéile* — no strength without unity.
- **Present-day beat:** the Book of Kells, and Pangur Bán's poem, survive.

### 3. Na Lochlannaigh — The Vikings (Dublin; 795–1014)

- **Anchor and reading:** Sitric Silkbeard and Dubh Linn. Read an annal/object-led
  account of a Norse-Gaelic king's town as it becomes a market, mint, and port.
- **Hook:** At the black pool (*Dubhlinn*), a Norse-Gaelic town grows into a market
  where Irish and Norse words travel with the goods.
- **Language:** past tense (regular + the must-have irregulars: *chuaigh, tháinig,
  rinne, chonaic*), directions and movement, market vocabulary, money and numbers
  11–20, Norse loanwords in Irish (*margadh*, *pingin*, *bróg*…), founding of the
  port towns.
- **Grammar notes:** the séimhiú as past-tense marker — the first mutation taught as
  *system*, not exception.
- **Artifact:** a silver arm-ring (hack-silver) + your nearest town's name decoded.
- **Present-day beat:** Dublin, Waterford, Wexford, Limerick — you've been saying
  Viking words your whole life.

### 4. Na Normannaigh — The Normans (Meath; 1169–1350)

- **Anchor and reading:** Hugh de Lacy and Trim Castle. Read a charter or chronicle
  excerpt alongside a plan of the castle at the ford.
- **Hook:** A castle rises over the ford at Trim. Its new lord and the people around
  him show how quickly conquest, settlement, and language become entangled.
- **Language:** possession (*tá capall agam* + the prepositional pronouns *agam/agat/
  aige/aici…*), describing people and places, adjective agreement and lenition after
  feminine nouns, Norman surnames (Fitz-, de Búrca, Buitléir).
- **Grammar notes:** Irish "has" no verb *to have* — and why *tá … agam* is the
  pattern behind half of Irish idiom (*tá ocras orm* teased for ch. 6).
- **Artifact:** your surname's origin card (Gaelic, Norse, Norman, or English — all
  are Irish stories).
- **Sensitivity:** first conquest chapter; frame through assimilation and mixing,
  which is the true story of the period.

### 5. Scoil na bhFilí — The Bardic World (1350–1600)

- **Hook:** Night in a bardic school: students compose in total darkness, verses held
  in memory. A chieftain pays richly for praise — and fears satire more than swords.
- **Language:** copula classification (*Is file é*, *Is bean láidir í*), comparatives
  and superlatives (*níos fearr, is fearr*), giving opinions, the seanfhocal
  tradition itself as content, terms for poetry and music.
- **Grammar notes:** the copula completes — identity, classification, and why *Is
  maith liom* is literally "it is good with me".
- **Artifact:** a two-line praise poem with your name woven in.
- **Present-day beat:** rap battles, slagging, and the enduring Irish belief that
  words are weapons — the bards never left.

### 6. Imeacht na nIarlaí — The Flight of the Earls (1550–1650)

- **Hook:** Rathmullan pier, September 1607. A ship waits. The old Gaelic order is
  leaving Ireland, and everyone on the shore knows something is ending.
- **Language:** past habitual (*bhíodh* — "things used to be"), feelings with *ar*
  (*tá brón orm, tá áthas orm*), leaving/journey vocabulary, the caoineadh (keen) as
  cultural form.
- **Grammar notes:** the emotion idiom — feelings sit *on* you in Irish, and what
  that says about how the language sees the self.
- **Artifact:** a sealed departure letter.
- **Sensitivity:** plantation follows this chapter's events. Tell it through named
  human beings on all sides; primary sources; no cartoon villains. Editorial
  principles (STRATEGY.md U7) apply from here through ch. 10.

### 7. Na Scoileanna Scairte — The Hedge Schools (1690–1780)

- **Hook:** A ditch-side classroom under penal law. The master teaches Latin, Greek,
  and sums — in Irish, for pennies, illegally. Learning as an act of defiance.
- **Language:** modals (*caithfidh mé*, *is féidir liom*, *ní mór dom*), forming
  negatives and questions across every tense learned so far, school and learning
  vocabulary.
- **Meta-resonance:** this is the chapter that speaks directly to re-learners — a
  people who risked everything to learn, mirrored against "I did 13 years and can't
  speak it". Handle school-system critique with warmth, not contempt.
- **Artifact:** a hedge-school hornbook.

### 8. An Gorta Mór — The Great Famine (1845–1852)

- **Hook:** Told through letters. A family in Conamara writes to a son in Boston;
  the letters get shorter; then they come from a different hand. The chapter the
  diaspora carries in its bones — and the wound that broke the language's spine.
- **Language:** reading and writing simple letters, family relations in depth, grief
  and hope, consolidation of all past-tense forms, emigration vocabulary
  (*imirce*, *long*, *slán*).
- **Artifact:** the learner writes their own short letter home in Irish — the first
  free composition in the course.
- **Sensitivity:** the heaviest chapter. No exercises gamified against suffering;
  review sessions in this chapter drop all playfulness. Historical framing
  (causes, responsibility) presented through sources and multiple historians'
  voices. This chapter is also *why* many users are here; treat it as sacred ground.

### 9. An Athbheochan — The Revival (1880–1916)

- **Hook:** A crowded Dublin hall, 1893. Douglas Hyde is founding Conradh na
  Gaeilge; a shop girl and a civil servant argue about whether a dying language can
  be willed back to life. Spoiler: you, the learner, are the answer.
- **Language:** future tense (*beidh, déanfaidh*), meetings and organising,
  persuading and promising, the vocabulary of hope; reading a line of the 1916
  Proclamation in Irish.
- **Artifact:** a period-style Conradh membership card with the learner's name.
- **Present-day beat:** Conradh na Gaeilge still exists; here's a branch near you.

### 10. Saoirse agus Scoilt — Freedom and Partition (1916–1949)

- **Hook:** Three voices across three decades — a rebel, a Belfast mill worker, a
  Free State schoolteacher — each answering: what did the new border mean for the
  language?
- **Language:** conditional (*dá mbeadh… / bheadh*  — "what might have been", the
  mood the era demands), civic vocabulary (saoránach, poblacht, teorainn), talking
  about North and South.
- **Sensitivity:** the most contested ground in the course. Multi-voice structure is
  mandatory, unionist perspective included honestly; the language belongs to nobody's
  flag (Presbyterian revivalists of the 1830s and Turas get foreshadowed here).
- **Artifact:** a 1937 constitution excerpt card — *Ós í an Ghaeilge an teanga
  náisiúnta…*

### 11. An Ghaeltacht (1900–today)

- **Hook:** A summer in Conamara — the learner's avatar finally *goes to the
  Gaeltacht*. Céilí nights, weather talk as social glue, sean-nós singing, and the
  discovery that real speech has accents.
- **Language:** the three dialects taught explicitly (how *Cén chaoi a bhfuil tú?*,
  *Conas atá tú?*, *Cad é mar atá tú?* map to one question), weather and sea
  vocabulary, music terms, informal registers, keeping a conversation going
  (*ab ea?*, *muise*, discourse noise).
- **Dialect note:** this is where the app's Connacht-first policy becomes visible
  content, and where Ulster/Munster audio (D2) pays off.
- **Artifact:** a verse of a sean-nós song, learned by ear.

### 12. Athbheochan Bhéal Feirste — The Belfast Revival (1969–today)

- **Hook:** From the Shaw's Road families who raised children in Irish with no state
  support, through the "jailtacht" learners of Long Kesh, to Linda Ervine's Turas in
  East Belfast — the most improbable revival story in Europe, still unfolding.
- **Language:** fast contemporary conversation, community and organising vocabulary,
  blessing/wish formulas (*go n-éirí leat*, *fad saoil agat*), Ulster forms
  introduced receptively [requires Ulster audio — gate on D2].
- **Sensitivity:** the north-star chapter (D1). Cross-community voices are the
  spine of it, not a sidebar. Build relationships with Turas/Glór na Móna before
  writing it; pay consultants from those communities.
- **Artifact:** a mural panel for the learner's museum.

### 13. Do Thurasa — Your Journey (today)

- **Hook:** The frame breaks: the final chapter is set in the learner's own life.
  Finding a ciorcal comhrá or Pop-Up Gaeltacht, surviving your first real
  conversation, choosing your next step (TEG A2/B1 exam, Oideas Gael week, weekly
  meetup). The last scene: in a museum, the learner stands before the stone they
  carved in Chapter 1.
- **Language:** full consolidation in conversational use; making plans; introducing
  yourself for real (the Chapter 1 payload, now fluent and unscripted).
- **Artifact:** An Fáinne — the ring worn to say *I speak Irish; speak it to me.*

## Open creative questions

- **Frame device:** what carries the learner between eras? Candidates: an unbroken
  thread motif; objects recurring across chapters (the ch. 1 stone reappearing in
  ch. 13 is already designed in); a bradán feasa (salmon of knowledge) guide
  character. Decide before ch. 2 production.
- **Chapter length tuning:** 13 chapters × 10 sessions is the planning number;
  the vertical slice (ch. 1, 5 sessions) exists to test whether sessions should be
  shorter/longer.
- **What we deliberately skipped:** pre-Ogham mythology (Táin, Fianna) — folded into
  chapters as told-stories (a monk copying the Táin in ch. 2, a bard reciting Fianna
  lore in ch. 5) rather than presented as history; Christianity's arrival is
  backdrop to ch. 1–2 rather than its own chapter. Revisit if learners ask for myth.
