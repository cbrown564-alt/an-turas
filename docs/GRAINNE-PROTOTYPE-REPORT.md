# Gráinne first-encounter prototype report

*11 July 2026 · iterative tester review · decision: approved as the foundation
for the next product-design phase*

## Executive conclusion

The tester approved the revised Gráinne first encounter after two substantial
corrections.

The successful version is deliberately small:

> island → Rockfleet and the stakes → meet Gráinne → find her name and family in the
> 1593 letter → use *Is mise…* → return to the wider island

It now feels trustworthy, clear enough, emotionally engaging, and recognisably an
invitation to learn about Ireland. The decisive change was to make Gráinne the subject
and the document part of her unfolding story. Irish now grows from the importance of
names instead of arriving as a lesson attached to the end.

This approves the **foundation**, not a full Gráinne story, the four-tab atlas, the
32-county progression, or the production content model. Before another tester round,
the team should answer the product questions described below and storyboard how this
simple encounter expands without becoming a dossier, database, or sequence of
caveats.

## What was tested

The review examined whether a first-time learner could:

- enter through the island without first understanding a course architecture;
- become interested in Gráinne as a person with place, power, loss, and agency;
- encounter a surviving record without the experience becoming an archive tutorial;
- feel the distinction between the record and later storytelling without being taught
  an evidence taxonomy;
- meet Irish inside the story and leave with one usable phrase;
- finish with a sense that Ireland had opened rather than that a lesson had ended.

It did not test historical or Irish-language sign-off, audio quality, retention over
time, a complete twenty-word payload, county completion, return scheduling,
subscription placement, or the wider atlas navigation after the encounter.

## Iterations and findings

### 1. The expansive atlas prototype exposed the system

The first build presented tabs, map modes, future routes, dossiers, evidence labels,
collection shelves, field notes, source states, and an eight-register story. The
tester could see the team's editorial and product architecture but could not easily
experience one coherent invitation.

**Finding:** first run must be authored. The product can reveal its breadth only after
the learner has a reason to care about it.

### 2. The simplified prototype became dry

The next build reduced the flow to a clear golden path, but it overcorrected toward
trust. Gráinne became an introduction to a document, followed by caution statements,
followed by a small amount of Irish. It felt formal and technically responsible but
not emotionally compelling.

**Finding:** accuracy is not a narrative register. Trust should be created by what the
story shows, the order in which it shows it, and careful affirmative writing. It
should not dominate the learner-facing voice.

### 3. The story-led prototype restored the invitation

The approved version begins at Rockfleet with concrete stakes: Gráinne's fleet,
property, and imprisoned son. It then gives her a dedicated beat as a leader shaped by
Clew Bay before the 1593 letter appears as the consequence of her decision to go to
court. Finding “Grany ne Maly” makes the source active and connects naturally to her
Irish name and the learner's *Is mise…*.

The last tonal correction treats later storytelling affirmatively:

> Later stories tell of Gráinne refusing to bow to Elizabeth. The letter holds its
> own act of defiance: she crossed from Clew Bay to the heart of English power and
> made the state answer her.

**Finding:** the product can be careful without sounding corrective. Later stories
can be surfaced as part of Gráinne's afterlife while the documentary story retains its
own force.

## Approved product principles

1. **A person, place, and unresolved situation open the story.** Metadata and course
   structure do not.
2. **One narrative movement per screen.** A screen may establish place, deepen a
   person, reveal evidence, or ask the learner to respond; it should not explain the
   whole product model.
3. **Evidence changes the story.** It appears when it reveals a name, relationship,
   decision, voice, or consequence—not merely to demonstrate trustworthiness.
4. **Trust is structural.** Use source-grounded writing, sequencing, and quiet source
   access. Reserve explicit labels for moments when the distinction materially changes
   meaning.
5. **Uncertainty is written affirmatively.** Say what a source carries and what later
   stories remember. Avoid leading with “does not,” “unsupported,” or “the prototype
   cannot.”
6. **Irish is inside the dramatic idea.** Gráinne's name crossing languages earns the
   learner's first act of self-identification. Future language should arise from the
   same kind of meaningful hinge.
7. **Progressive disclosure protects the invitation.** The atlas, collection, review,
   evidence, and return systems may be deep, but they are revealed only when the
   learner has earned a use for them.
8. **The learner remains themselves.** Interaction can involve looking, listening,
   tracing, choosing, speaking, and remembering without pretending the learner caused
   history or witnessed unattested dialogue.

## The next product questions

These questions should be answered before extending the implementation or returning
to testers.

### Story scope and shape

- Is the first complete story the 1593 journey and negotiation, or a broader account
  of Gráinne's life organised around that journey?
- What must a learner understand about Gaelic maritime power, family, Bingham, the
  Tudor state, the audience, the outcome, and Gráinne's later life?
- Which four to six narrative movements form a satisfying arc, and where are the
  natural stopping and returning points?
- What is the emotional question that pulls the learner from one movement to the next?

### The learner's role

- What meaningful action does the learner take in each movement?
- How often should the experience ask for attention rather than an answer?
- Which interactions produce discovery—finding a name, following a coast, comparing
  wording, hearing a place-name—and which merely decorate exposition?

### Language integration

- Which useful Irish genuinely belongs to this story: identity, origin, family,
  asking, answering, sea, place, power, and return?
- Is twenty words the right promise for one county story, or should it emerge across
  multiple encounters in a county?
- Where does each phrase first matter, how is it used by the learner, and when does it
  return in a new context?
- How much explicit grammar is helpful before it interrupts narrative momentum?

### Evidence and later storytelling

- Which document should be the primary learner-facing source: the 1593 questionnaire,
  correspondence, the September letter, or a small sequence of them?
- What can the learner actually read, hear, find, or compare in each source?
- At what depth do citations, transcription, translation, rights, and scholarly notes
  appear?
- How should bowing, Latin, hair-cutting, marriage, “pirate queen,” and other later or
  disputed stories enter as afterlife without becoming either fact or a warning list?

### Product container

- Is a full story one continuous encounter, several short episodes, or a mixture?
- When does the wider island open, and what is the next obvious invitation once it
  does?
- What single thing enters the collection after each episode: evidence, a phrase, a
  personal response, or some combination?
- What gives the learner a reason to return before a spaced-repetition system asks
  them to?
- Which parts of the current four-destination atlas are necessary after one story, and
  which should wait until the product has enough content to justify them?

### Repeatability and production

- Which parts of this encounter are a reusable story grammar, and which are uniquely
  Gráinne's?
- What is the minimum source, rights, historian, Irish-language, audio, and visual
  package required before a story enters production?
- Can this level of narrative craft be sustained across launch stories without an
  unaffordable bespoke interface for every county?

## Plan: build the full story without rebuilding the complexity

### Step 1 — decide the story contract

Write a one-page answer to the product questions above. Define the story's scope,
length, episode model, learner role, language promise, evidence depth, completion
meaning, and post-story destination. Record explicit non-goals.

### Step 2 — build a source-backed narrative spine

**Scope decision (D15):** test a selective account of Gráinne's broader life, with
the 1593 crisis as its organising centre. Do not build either a 1593-only document
lesson or an exhaustive cradle-to-grave biography.

The selection question for every movement is:

> Can Gráinne preserve her family, authority, and way of life as Tudor power closes
> around Clew Bay?

Each movement must change the learner's answer. Treat that way of life as a concrete
maritime, kinship, economic, and political order, not a romanticised innocence.

Package the arc as four to six episodes of roughly 8–12 minutes. Each episode must
have a satisfying local movement, an authored exit, and a hook into the next change.
Make the next episode available immediately. The arc is bingeable, but every episode
must also support a satisfying stop and an authored return. Do not impose timers or
artificial waiting.

Every core episode must include one meaningful historical discovery and one
meaningful use of Irish tied to the dramatic stakes. Observation may occupy the rest
of the episode, but decorative taps, immediate sentence-check questions, and entirely
observational episodes do not meet the contract.

Every source carrying a material claim must have a three-level evidence ladder:
story view, optional closer look, and full editorial record. The story must work at
the first level; transcription, translation, context, and uncertainty belong in the
second; citations, rights, claim ledger, notes, and review history belong in the third.

Use one collection hierarchy: handled evidence accumulates quietly in Mayo's county
record; earned Irish accumulates in usable language; one personal artifact arrives
only on completion of the whole county arc. Do not expose these as equal collectible
systems. Mayo's completion artifact is a learner-authored voyage chart tracing the
Clew Bay–London–Mayo movement, joining Gráinne's name to the learner's Irish
self-identification and restrained marks from evidence they actually handled. It is
not a replica historical document.

Create a six-column storyboard before more SwiftUI work:

| Movement | Historical change | Human stake | Evidence that reveals it | Irish earned | Learner action |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Aim for four to six movements. A provisional sequence to test—not yet a content
decision—is:

1. Clew Bay and the world that made Gráinne's power possible;
2. Gráinne as a leader of family, castles, boats, and alliances;
3. Bingham's pressure and the losses that force a decision;
4. the crossing to London and the family named in the records;
5. negotiation, response, and what changed;
6. return to Mayo, later storytelling, and the coast today.

### Step 3 — make a language weave, not a vocabulary list

Map each proposed Irish item to its first dramatic need, learner use, later reuse, and
audio requirement. Remove any word that exists only to satisfy a quota. Prototype the
transitions where history turns toward the learner.

### Step 4 — design the evidence ladder

For every source, define a learner-depth view, an optional closer look, and a full
editorial record. Keep rights and review state out of the narrative surface. Decide
how later stories enter through comparison, afterlife, place, and memory rather than
through caveat cards.

### Step 5 — storyboard in low fidelity

Test pacing, copy, interaction, and return points as a lightweight storyboard. Do not
add atlas modes, entity systems, collection taxonomies, or new global navigation while
the full story contract is unsettled.

### Step 6 — internal product review, then implementation

Review the storyboard against four gates:

- emotionally compelling without invented history;
- Gráinne is understood as a person, not merely a source subject;
- Irish is memorable because it matters inside the story;
- the learner always knows why they are looking, acting, or continuing.

Only after those gates pass should the team expand the SwiftUI prototype.

### Step 7 — next tester round

Test the complete story arc rather than more atlas breadth. A successful learner
should be able to retell the central movement, name several Mayo places and human
stakes, explain what the record revealed without repeating interface labels, use the
Irish in a new introduction, and want to continue into another Irish story.

## Decision

Keep the approved first encounter as the design floor. Do not expand it by restoring
the removed architecture. The next milestone is a resolved product contract and a
source-backed full-story storyboard, followed by internal review. Wider external
testing comes after those decisions.
